## Abstract

This project proposes a design of a waverider, a special class of hypersonic vehicle that
‘rides’ on an attached shock wave to generate more lift. This project aims to propose a new
design that mitigates the performance loss during off-design conditions, make a Python
script to rapidly generate waverider bodies, visualize waveriders in 3D, and to make
publicly available for verification and reproducibility, the design parameters chosen in all
designs presented in this report. Background information will be covered such as the
historical origins, types of flow fields and the kinds of waveriders they generate, and the
methods of applying the osculating cone theory in their generation, while addressing the
lack of availability of design parameters. The methodology involves solving the Taylor-
Maccoll equation and then applying the osculating plane theory to generate waveriders
that are then combined into one parallel waverider. Aerodynamic data is calculated using
Newtonian Impact Theory and is compared with a different but similar design.

## 1. Introduction

This project is involved in a class of hypersonic vehicles called waveriders which are
designed to create a shock wave that is totally attached to the leading edge of the body (Son
et al., 2022). This creates a high-pressure region on the lower surface compared to the upper
surface which generates lift.

Waveriders are used in weapon delivery systems because they can take a lower altitude
journey to target, while avoiding radar detection for longer than traditional ICBMs. They can
also be used to increase the influence of the Oberth effect during gravity assists for
interplanetary travel as the aerodynamic body is designed to maximize lift to drag ratio in
hypersonic flow regimes (Son et al., 2022), turning the manoeuvre into an aerogravity assist
as the periapsis can be lowered into a planet’s atmosphere. This also results in significant
fuel savings.

Each cone-derived waverider is optimized for a single cruise speed. This means as the
waverider deviates from the desired cruise speed, its performance will deteriorate (Liu et al.,
2019). This is not ideal as for a huge portion of the waverider’s journey, it would be
underperforming. This project seeks to solve this issue by merging two cone-derived
waveriders into one design, creating inboard and outboard leading edges for a shock wave
to attach to, thus allowing the waverider body to perform as intended at two different speeds.

This project aims to use Python to create a Taylor-Maccoll cone equation solver, implement
the osculating cone method to generate two waverider wireframes optimized for freestream
Mach numbers 6 and 13, calculate the lift to drag ratio for varying angles of attack and
freestream Mach numbers and validate these designs by comparing them to the data
generated by Jun Liu et al in their paper on another waverider design methodology (Liu et al.,
2019), create a design that merges two cone-derived waveriders and to calculate the lift to
drag ratio across a range of freestream Mach numbers. This project also aims to make
available all the design parameters and settings chosen for the purpose of reproducibility.

### 1.1 Objectives

- To create, entirely in Python, a new design for a parallel waverider that mitigates the
    performance loss during off design conditions
- To create code that rapidly generates waverider bodies
- To show as a proof of concept that a waverider may be derived from any number of
    waveriders
- Make available all design parameters and settings used for the design proposal


## 2. Literature review

### 2.1 Historical Background

Waveriders were introduced by T. R. F. Nonweiler in his paper ‘Aerodynamic Problems of
Manned Space Vehicles’ in September 1959 specifically to tackle the peak deceleration and
peak heating issues that occur during atmospheric re-entry that are beyond what a typical
human can survive (Nonweiler, 1959). He stated that the addition of lift can decrease the
peak deceleration experienced by a passenger, as shown in Figure 1.

_Figure 1 graph showing Nonweiler's theory that high L/D lowers peak deceleration during re-entry (Nonweiler, 1959)_

He proposed two designs based on supersonic wedge flow that were all-wing and no
fuselage because of the expectation of high wing loading and had a ‘caret’ shape or were
composites of caret shaped waveriders as seen in his ‘W’. These shapes were intended to
capture the shockwave produced underneath the waverider body, hence giving the concept
its name, which provides the high-pressure region required to improve lift-to-drag in the
supersonic flow regime.


_Figure 2 Diagrams of Nonweiler's 2D planar flow derived waveriders. (a) shows his 'W' waverider design, while (b) shows
his caret waverider design (Nonweiler, 1959)_

In 1990, Sobieczky et al introduced osculating cone theory which assumes that three-
dimensional flow may be locally approximated to be axisymmetric flow (Sobieczky et al.,
1990), that is to say that it can be assumed to follow the conical flow regime at each point
from the leading edge onwards in each osculating plane. Figure 3 shows one osculating
plane being drawn from the principal axis that cuts through the flow field. The flow through
that plane is assumed to be conical for that plane. Performance characteristics predicted
by this method were confirmed via CFD analysis and the ability for waveriders derived of
this method were confirmed to contain the shock wave well (Sobieczky et al., 1990).


_Figure 3 Left diagram shows Sobieczky's novel osculating cone theory being used to generate a lower surface (right)
optimised for M=4 and shock angle=18 degrees (Sobieczky et al., 1990)_

### 2.2 Types of Waveriders

Waveriders are technically any aerodynamic body that can ride on its own attached shock
wave, so it does not necessarily have to be derived from any one flow field. Having said that,
waveriders are typically derived from either a supersonic conical flow field (an inviscid 2D
axisymmetric flow model) as seen in Figure 5, or a supersonic wedge flow field (an inviscid
2D planar flow model) as seen in Figure 4 , which are often referred to as caret waveriders.

_Figure 4 Diagram showing a caret waverider derived from a wedge flow field (Ding et al., 2017)_


_Figure 5 Diagram showing a typical cone derived waverider (Ding et al., 2017)_

Most modern waverider designs are derived from conical flow fields due to their improved
volumetric efficiency (Ding et al., 2017), and therefore, this project too uses this flow field
for its design proposal.

This study is focused on combined design waveriders where two waveriders, designed for
specific Mach numbers, are joined together in a way to mitigate the performance loss
during off design conditions, such as when the incoming flow speed is not the design Mach
number or, the angle of attack changes. Such a waverider may be constructed in many
ways. Within the category of cone derived waveriders, there are two ways: tandem and
parallel. Tandem waveriders are constructed by connecting the high-speed body, which is
generally smaller and sharper in shape, in ‘series’ using a straight connecting section to
the low-speed body. The parallel waverider, which this project is designing, creates its
body by replacing points from the low-speed surface wherever the high-speed surface
overlaps. This results in two distinct leading edges for the shockwave to attach to, which
may mitigate the performance loss.

When designing a tandem waverider, the features of the connecting section must be
decided by the user, resulting in added complexity for the designer and in creating the code
that can generate such a waverider. For this reason, this project is proposing a design that
is a parallel waverider.

Even more complicated design concepts include double waveriders that, similar to
Nonweiler’s ‘W’ caret waverider, use two identical lower surfaces cut into a single body,
each generating its own shockwave to generate lift. Another is the star waverider which
consists of several caret waveriders joined together to form a star cross section.


### 2.3 Applications

_Figure 6 A sketch showing the conical roll technique for aerogravity assists (Lunan, 2015)_

One potential application is for fuel-efficient space travel. The Oberth effect is where a
spacecraft executing a manoeuvre is most fuel-efficient which is at the point of maximum
velocity, which occurs at the periapsis, or point of closest approach, to a celestial body
(Bailer-Jones, 2021). This effect was used by Voyager 1 in many fly-by encounters with
planets and moons to achieve incredibly high speeds to escape the solar system. The
issue with pure gravity assists using the Oberth effect is that there is a limit to how much
you can change the trajectory angle during such a manoeuvre due to the planet’s
atmosphere. However, a waverider can aid in this issue by taking advantage of the planet’s
atmosphere, as aerodynamic forces can increase the bending angle by any amount
(Johnson & Longuski, 2002), provided the spacecraft can survive, using the ‘Conical Roll’
technique (Lunan, 2015) shown in Figure 6. The velocities a waverider may experience
while travelling through a planet’s atmosphere or during re-entry may exceed Mach 25 so a
waverider designed for this needs to generate sufficient lift for a range of Mach numbers as
it decelerates due to drag. While gravity assists are possible without burning fuel, an
aerogravity assist for the purpose of achieving extreme trajectory change, may have to burn
some fuel to overcome the drag force due to the extra time spent inside the atmosphere.


Another more terrestrial application of waveriders is in military payload delivery which can
include ultra-fast equipment transportation to even nuclear warheads. The advantage in
nuclear payload delivery over traditional intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs) is that
the waverider missile spends most of its time out of enemy radar range, due to the
curvature of the Earth, whereas an ICBM takes a high altitude, spending most of its time
within enemy radar range. The use of waveriders in nuclear payload delivery can drastically
reduce response times. A waverider optimized for this task will be designed for a lower
range of Mach numbers, typically in the 5 to 10 range.

### 3.4 Osculating Cone Method

The technique used to generate the wireframe for a waverider body is the osculating cone
method which uses several, equally spaced by angle, osculating planes that start from the
principal axis and intersect with the shock cone. A few design parameters must be chosen.
The cone body radius can be arbitrarily set to 1m, the maximum angle of the osculating
planes can be between 0° and 90° and the number of planes, that is one of two variables
that determines the resolution of the lower surface.


_Figure 11 Code that sets the design parameters: cone radius, max angle of osculating planes, number of planes and
radius of shock cone_

The rounding function used in line 5 is needed to fix an issue with floating point
calculations that Python does not handle well without. The default number of decimal
places is 15, which caused the code to unnecessarily round values, so this function
reduces the number of decimal places to 14, which is still beyond the required level of
precision.

Then, in the yz plane (back-plane) the points of intersection that form a circular arc in the
back plane and the shock cone are found and given the designation of P2. Then, the upper
surface of the waverider’s back plane is arbitrarily chosen to take the shape of a negative
quadratic which is given the designation of P1. P1 and P2 should intersect, and those
points of intersection determine their limits.


_Figure 12 Code that defines the shock arc and the shape of the upper surface in the back plane_

The x-coordinates of P1 are then subtracted to meet the surface of the shock cone in the xy
plane and form the leading-edge points, P3, so that the upper surface is parallel to the
freestream, which avoids any drag contributions from the non-lifting surface (Broglia,
2016).

_Figure 13 Code that finds the leading edge by projecting upper surface onto shock cone_

The points from the leading edge then follow along the streamlines after the shock and end
on the back plane again forming the points P4.

However, a simpler method is used to approximate the shape of the lower surface by
assuming that the lines P3 to P4 form follow a line parallel to the surface of the cone body.
This is known as the aforementioned straight-line approximation method. The assumption
is based on the idea that for a long enough streamline, the angle to the freestream
becomes close to the angle of the cone body. This project uses this method as the
streamline tracing was unsuccessful in its implementation due to an error in the way P1
was defined, which will be discussed further in the Results section.


_Figure 14 Code that finds the lower surface in the back plane using straight-line approximation_

Then each of the four design lines are broken down into three one-dimensional lists
containing the x, y, and z co-ordinates separately.

### 3.5 Lower Surface Points by Straight-Line Approximation

This algorithm is done in cartesian co-ordinates to take advantage of the changes that only
occur in the xy plane.

_Figure 15 Straight-line approximation algorithm. The 'dx' term determines the streamwise resolution_

This algorithm is another iteratively solved method that starts at the points along the
leading edge and takes constant steps in the x direction. The angle in the flow field is a
constant value equal to the cone body angle, which is then fed into the Taylor-Maccoll
solution to extract the velocity components at each point on the lower surface. Since the
velocity along a ray of constant angle to the principal axis is the same at all points on that
ray, the points on the lower surface all have the same velocity components, simplifying the
process of getting aerodynamic data.

### 3.7 Generating Combined Waverider

This entire process so far must be repeated for a second, slower Mach number waverider to
have its own lower surface defined a stored in an array. Once the lower surfaces are
obtained, they can be combined to produce the parallel waverider that this project is
proposing. The transformations to the surfaces that are to be performed will mean that the
aerodynamic characteristics can no longer be found within the conical flow model.

The design parameters for creating this are the points where the faster surface replaces the
slower surface and the translations needed to connect the two surfaces together.

The approach taken was to first decide where that replacement occurs. For this specific
design 10% of the distance from each end of the slower surface in the z direction was chosen.
Then the points on the faster surface where they are outside those replacement points are
removed. The points on the slower surface where they are inside the replacement points are


also removed. At this point the two surfaces are still at different angles and so it is
impossible to connect them together. Therefore, a rotation matrix is applied to the faster
surface to match the angle of incidence of the slower surface. Finally, the faster surface
points are translated into position so that the two surfaces form distinct leading edges.
These translations do not affect the method of obtaining aerodynamic characteristics, only
the rotations and the cutting of points do as they change the angle of incidence and the
individual areas of the component surfaces.

#### 3.7.1 Newtonian Impact Theory

Since this combined waverider cannot rely on the solution of the Taylor-Maccoll cone
equation, a different approach is required. Isaac Newton came up with a theory for lift
generation by proposing that it was the result of a reaction force from the tangential
deflection of fluid particles along a flat plate. He assumed no interaction between particles
and no random particle motion (Anderson, 2006). While invalid for subsonic flow regimes
due to the lack of random motion, Newton’s model is very applicable for the supersonic flow
regime and becomes more accurate at higher speeds.

_Figure 16 Diagram showing the mechanics of Newtonian Impact Theory. The incoming stream is deflected tangentially by
the inclined flat plate (Anderson, 2006)_


_Figure 17 Diagram showing the 'shadow' effect in Newton's model. These surfaces experience no pressure (Anderson,
2006)_

The path taken by the fluid particles after being deflected is always a straight line in
Newton’s model, which means that surfaces that are in the ‘shadow’ of the freestream do
not experience any pressure, which means that those surfaces have a coefficient of
pressure of zero. The surfaces that do experience pressure have their coefficient of pressure
calculated by the following equation (Anderson, 2006):

```
𝑐𝑝= 2 sin^2 (𝜃)
Eq1 4
```

### 4.3 Discussion

_Figure 22 Graph showing Lift-to-Drag variance with Angle of Attack_


In Figure 22 the lines show lift-to-drag ratio over a range of angles of attack as predicted by
Newtonian Impact Theory, while the star marks show the lift-to-drag at 0º incidence as
predicted by the Taylor-Maccoll conical model. The higher speed waverider has a
significantly higher lift-to-drag ratio at 0º incidence, possibly due to the previously
mentioned compaction of the streamlines inside the shock cone. The combined waverider
shows a performance improvement over all angles of attack as compared to the slower
speed waverider but is less performant than the higher speed waverider over all angles of
attack. This means to match the lift to drag ratio of the higher speed waverider, the
combined waverider can maintain a lower angle of attack.

The higher speed waverider shows a rapid decrease in lift-to-drag as angle of attack
increases above 0º, whereas this is not the case for the combined waverider, which may
be beneficial for a waverider designed for aerogravity assists as the angle of attack may be
changing during the conical roll through an atmosphere as this is a much more robust
design and a loss in performance could mean a loss in altitude putting the waverider body
in a higher density region where it will experience greater heating and a greater loss in
velocity.

For this specific combined waverider, it does not show a large improvement over the
slower waverider, but that may be due to the design parameters chosen and so a better
design is likely to exist that does not have this issue. Having said that, the loss in lift-to-
drag ratio as angle of attack increases is less than the slower waverider as shown by the
increasing gap between the blue and purple lines at around 0.2 rad angle of attack.

### 4.4 Limitations of Methodology and Suggestions

In implementing the waverider generator in Python, an attempt was made to apply the
streamline tracing method but it did not seem to give good results. Surfaces were generated
that were not attached to the leading edge and were not shaped like the surfaces found in
other papers. Since it could not be qualitatively verified, the attempt was abandoned. Had
this method been implemented successfully, the variety of the shapes of the lower surfaces
would increase and become more interesting, not to mention the obvious increase in the
volumetric efficiency of the waveriders being generated as opposed to using the straight-
line approximation.

An interesting point to raise with respect to the Taylor-Maccoll solver, is that despite taking
steps that are on the order of 1e-7 radians, the solution is only precise to 5 significant figures.
It is unclear at the moment as to why this occurs. Earlier in the project, the plan was to learn
and implement the Runge-Kutta method that is recommended to use as an iterative method
to solve, but the Euler finite difference method was used instead due to how intuitive it was
to learn and ease of implementation, so perhaps the loss in precision is due to this decision.

The way the code uses arrays and tuples is certainly suboptimal and can be more efficient
to reduce the run time. For example, the array containing the co-ordinates of the lower
surface may appear as an Nx3 matrix but it is in fact an array of many 1x3 lists. This made it
exceedingly difficult to calculate lift and drag as each osculating plane contains an unknown
number of points. This run time impact is felt during the generation of the combined lower
surface as the vast majority of the cells are dealing with operations on arrays that could be
potentially 100,000s of points long, which affects the ability of the code to meet the
requirement for rapid waverider generations. This use of arrays makes it impossible to
extract points that lie on a line that runs spanwise, including the points in the back plane,
which impacts the code’s ability to meet the visualization objective.

Another visualization limitation is the lack of plotting the upper surface. Since it was
decided that for the sake of simplicity that the upper surface be parallel to the freestream,
it does not contribute to the generation of lift in the Taylor-Maccoll model (also because in
that model the angle of attack is set to 0) nor in the Newtonian Impact model since for all


angles of attack, the upper surface remains in the shadow of the flow. Thus, the plotting of
the upper surface was cut and deemed an extra in case there was remaining time.

The biggest problem this methodology raises is seen in the gaps left in the combined
waverider, best shown in Figure 20b, as it is once again a direct result of how arrays and
tuples were used in this project. Since the number of points in each line for both surfaces
always changes for different design parameters it is impossible to predict which nth value of
the array lies in a particular point in 3D space for each design parameter determined
iteration.

These gaps result in missing points that would contribute to the generation of pressure,
which may explain why in Figure 22 the performance increase over the slow waverider is less
than expected.

Therefore, the way this code uses arrays and tuples must be changed. A suitable suggestion
would be to use np.array as it allows for matrix operations to be done much faster. Also, the
issue of the gap is solved by storing the points in a 3D matrix so that specific points of interest
can be called by knowing which osculating plane (let the number of planes be p) and where
in that osculating plane the point lies (let the number of points in each plane be n), thereby
creating a matrix with dimensions p x n x 3. This would improve the process of combining the
two lower surfaces by allowing for a more appropriate way to replace the points on the
slower surface with points on the faster surface.

The assumption of the ratio of specific heats being 1.4, and that the specific gas constant of
air being 287 J/(kgK) throughout the code hampers its applicability to the design of
waveriders intended to perform aerogravity assists as the atmospheric conditions of other
planets vary wildly compared to the Earth. This is easily fixed through the use of global
variables that can be set by the user at the start.

## 5. Conclusion and Further Steps

Waveriders are suited for fast suborbital payload delivery and for efficient space travel, both
applications involve the hypersonic flow regime, due to their ability to capture a shock wave
to improve its lift-to-drag ratio. There are many ways to generate waveriders, though modern
designs tend to be based on the conical flow model and by applying some variation of the
osculating cone theory, but the literature often lacks sufficient design parameters to
replicate these designs, and so this project has revealed in detail all of the settings and input
decisions made to propose a new waverider, which was one of the objectives.


This project succeeds in some of its objectives and can improve to meet the other objectives.
The code demonstrates its abilities to rapidly generate both cone derived waveriders and
parallel waveriders and also to visualize them in three dimensions. The Taylor-Maccoll
solver was successfully implemented and verified by comparing the output to an online flow
calculator. Osculating cone theory was used to generate the conical waveriders and were
qualitatively verified.

However, the main goal to propose a design that mitigates the performance loss during off
design conditions requires more work to meet, although this project has definitely made
strides in the right direction. The results have shown improvements in some aerodynamic
characteristics but more data is required to give a full analysis.

Once the limitations with the methodology are dealt with, this project can be taken further
by creating a graphical user interface to make changing settings and generating waveriders
much easier. In order to meet the main goal, data must be gathered that shows lift-to-drag
ratio variation for a range of freestream Mach numbers. Due to lift-to-drag ratio being
independent from Mach number in Newtonian Impact Theory, it is unclear at this time how
this data should be gathered. Perhaps a different method such as the Tangent Cone may
solve this issue. This project can be further improved by plotting the upper surface and the
back plane. By obtaining all the surface points, the waverider body may be exported to fluid
simulation software such as Ansys for further aerodynamic analysis and to SolidWorks for
creating engineering drawings to manufacture waveriders for wind tunnel testing.
